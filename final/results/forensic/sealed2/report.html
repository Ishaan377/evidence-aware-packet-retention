What this test shows

EBR led every other method on attack signature recall at every cap. At 5% it kept 94.4%, against 85.9% for IDS-triggered capture with fill, 79.9% for rare destinations and 77.5% for the tuned cutoff. Its lead over IDS-triggered capture clears the 95% interval at 5% and 10%, but not at 1% and 2%.

This test did not exercise the main fix. Payload-free flows would have taken at most 3.4% of the packet budget on these captures, far below the 30% cap, so the cap never engaged. On the capture that exposed the flaw they would take 82%. The two versions therefore tie on signature recall in every case, and the evidence for the cap comes only from data that was not sealed.

The SMB change made no clear difference here. With the cap idle, alert instance recall moved by -1.2, -1.6, -0.9 and +0.6 points at the four caps. That is within scan noise: scanning identical selections twice changed a case by up to 4.5 points and the mean case by 1.2. An earlier version of this report called this a small cost, which went further than the evidence.

What changed

The first sealed test showed EBR losing about 40% of attack signatures on a human red-team capture. Investigating that capture led to two changes.

Payload-free flows now get at most 30% of the budget. On that capture, scan flows with no payload took 186 MB of a 227 MB packet budget, because they are cheap enough to outbid real evidence. Their value is unchanged and any budget left over goes back to them, so captures without a scan storm are barely affected.

SMB is now treated as readable. EBR used to discount its later bytes as if they were encrypted.

How this was tested

The new version was frozen on 2026-10-30 at 22:25:54, with hashes of 16 files, before any sealed capture was opened. Only sizes and format headers were read. The sealed cases were chosen by a fixed rule before the fix was written: consecutive files from the middle of each event. They come from three WRCCDC events, two CTU networks with live infections, and one unused CIC day.

The first version ran on the same captures. Its replay was checked first: on 12 earlier comparisons it matched the original results exactly, in both selections and scores.

Disclosure. The 2026 capture uses a link type EBR could not read, found from its header before the freeze, so support for it was added. This changes nothing for other captures, and both versions use the same parser.

The first sealed test

The first version was tested on 11.3 GB of different captures. It caught 8 of 8 labelled attacks at a 5% cap and kept 91.7% of attack signatures, against 86.5% for IDS-triggered capture with fill. That test also found the weakness on human red-team traffic that led to this version.

Prototype: verify before delete

These results are not sealed. Their outcomes could be compared with earlier results on the same captures, and they come from a separate prototype, not the frozen version. The loop's settings were fixed before this run and were not tuned afterwards.

A reduction is normally a blind bet, but the original still exists until it is deleted. So the prototype scans both copies with Suricata, puts back the flows behind any lost detection, and repeats up to twice, all inside the same byte budget. Repair here used only rules published before each capture, the same knowledge given to IDS-triggered capture. Means cover 11 cases.

Gain. With repair, signature recall beat IDS-triggered capture at every cap, and its 95% intervals exclude zero. Alert instances are clearly better at 5% and 10%. They are lower at 1% and 2%, where the intervals include zero.

Cost. Repair used 9.3%, 6.2%, 2.7% and 0.9% of the budget at the four caps, and it requires scanning the original before deletion. It made 6 of 88 case comparisons worse, by up to 15 points at a 1% cap, because repaired flows displace other evidence. 33 of 1118 usable alerts stayed lost at 5%, mostly rate-based rules that need many flows.

Blind-spot estimate. Hiding half the rule families from repair predicted recall on later-published rules to within 9.1 points on average, against 26.8 for guessing the mean, across 40 case and cap pairs. Its worst miss was 42 points.

Earlier claims that were wrong

Scan repeatability was overstated. An early check found identical alerts on complete captures. Reduced captures vary more: 9 of 20 identical selections scored differently when scanned twice, by up to 4.5 points of alert instance recall on one case. Signature recall did not change in those comparisons, though the third test later found that it can. Rate-based rules running across threads are the likely source. Instance-recall differences of a point or two in these reports should not be read as real.

SMB was only part of the cause. The first report blamed the SMB discount. Testing showed that the budget lost to scan flows mattered more. SMB still helps, so both changes were kept.

Gaps were not the cause of the drop at 10%. The first report suggested that gaps inside flows stopped the IDS parser. The affected login flows alert at every fidelity level when scanned alone, so that explanation was wrong. The drop disappeared once scan flows were capped.

A steeper discount looked like the fix and was not. It rescued the red-team capture but halved instance recall on an infiltration capture. Testing it on all 13 earlier cases exposed this, and the budget cap replaced it.

Limits

Lossless gzip on these captures reaches 56.5% of the original size and loses nothing.

5 scored cases cannot show general superiority, and the intervals above reflect that.

The WRCCDC slices are short, about a minute each, and they have no attack labels here. Only one labelled attack could be scored at all.

The sealed slices contained no scan storm, so a further sealed test on scan-heavy captures is needed before claiming the cap works in general.
